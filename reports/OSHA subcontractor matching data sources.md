# Chase identifiers, not fuzzier name matching

Site Safety Intelligence will match more subcontractors correctly by collecting identifiers and fixing its evaluation than by refining string similarity, and the five highest-ranked moves cost little beyond engineering time. The headline 0.85 precision is mostly a labelling artefact, because **17 of the 18 silver-label "false" matches are same-family pairs** such as D.R. Horton divisions, while the real gaps sit where the labels barely look: **none of 15 different-name true pairs matched and 8 never became candidates**, and only 6% of establishments under 20 employees can carry a label at all. The cheapest data wins are already on disk: the California licence file holds about 45,800 of the CSLB's roughly 179,000 licences (California links 13% of OSHA establishments to a licence against Washington's 57%), Washington's link rules miss firms already in its licence file because of zip mismatches, abbreviations and renames, and 21,690 Washington inspections carry an L&I inspection number that a public endpoint confirms against a candidate UBI. Among new sources, live probes found 87% of sampled employers in New York's free corporation registry and name-history tables, about 55% in the Texas Comptroller's franchise and sales-tax files, about 37% in Washington prevailing-wage filings and about one in five in FMCSA's carrier census. Free national files that pair DBAs with legal names and EINs (WHD enforcement, Form 5500, H-2B, SEC Exhibit 21) remain unmeasured. On methods, widen candidate generation with identifier and alias routes and add a RELATED outcome for corporate families before moving scoring to token-weighted Fellegi–Sunter and list-wise LLM adjudication. No source found sole proprietors (1 of 14 person-named employers turned up anywhere), so for them the product must rely on GC-entered licence numbers and GC confirmation. Paid databases, from D&B at $0.24 a match and $19.70 a family tree to Enigma at $1.50–$2.50 a lookup, should wait for a 200-name pilot.

## Free fixes outrank every purchase in the adoption order

The ranking weighs three things: how directly an item hits the six failure modes or the two headline numbers, how strong its evidence is, and what it costs. Evidence counts as strong when it was measured in this project's warehouse and live probes, weaker when it is only inferred from literature and documentation. Evaluation fixes come first because the current labels cannot see the cases the product exists for, so no later change could be shown to help. Fixes to data the project already holds follow. Then come free registries with measured coverage, alongside the method changes that turn their identifiers into candidates and scores, then free files nobody has measured against OSHA, and last the paid options, gated behind a pilot.

Every coverage figure here is a human-judged hit rate from 30 to 60 sampled establishments, with 95% intervals of roughly ±15–25 points. The New York, Texas and Washington rates also come from different states than the national ones. So gaps under about 20 points settle nothing ([coverage probes](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/source_coverage_probes.md)).

OpenCorporates' free tier is closed to this project because it excludes corporations ([OpenCorporates terms](https://opencorporates.com/terms-of-use-2/)).

## The 0.85 precision measures family labels, not matching mistakes

The silver labels call two establishments the same company when ITA links both to one EIN. They call them different when the two share a name core and state but carry different EINs ([run.py](../eval/matching/run.py)). So "different" means "different legal entity", which is not what a GC means by a different company. Of the 18 negatives the matcher matched, **17 are same-family pairs** under name, address or prefix heuristics, among them four D.R. Horton pairs, Hagerman, Lobar, Christman and Doran. Counting those as related rather than wrong raises matched precision from **0.845 to 0.99 (98 of 99)** ([local data checks](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/local_data_checks.md)). The labels even contradict each other. DR HORTON INC GREENSBORO in High Point and DR HORTON in Kingsport form a positive pair, while DR HORTON in Charlotte and DR HORTON in Raleigh form a negative one ([results.md](../eval/matching/results.md)). The family judgments are heuristic and one reviewer's reading, not checked against corporate records, but the direction is unambiguous: the matcher rarely confuses unrelated companies.

| Silver pair type (count) | Matched | Uncertain | Excluded | Not found |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Positive: same name, other address (135) | 98 | 37 | 0 | 0 |
| Positive: different name (15) | 0 | 5 | 2 | 8 |
| Negative: same core, different EIN (144) | 12 | 43 | 89 | 0 |
| Negative: same name, different EIN (6) | 6 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The table, from [results.md](../eval/matching/results.md), shows where matching actually falls short. All 98 matched positives are an identical name at another address, and so are **37 of the 42 uncertain positives (88%)**. The "28% uncertain" figure is therefore mostly multi-site firms whose branches the rules will not merge on name alone. Every candidate-recall miss and every false exclusion is a different-name pair. Of 15 such pairs, **none matched, 8 never became candidates** and 2 were excluded. So the 0.947 candidate recall hides roughly a 50% miss rate on exactly the DBA, rename and descriptor cases GCs struggle with. The larger adjudication samples confirm it: across seeds 7 and 11, **29 of 65 same-company, different-name pairs never became candidates** ([seed 7](../eval/adjudication/results.md); [seed 11](../eval/adjudication/results-seed11.md)).

The sample also describes the wrong population ([local data checks](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/local_data_checks.md)). Label-eligible establishments have a median OSHA headcount of 14, against 5 for the population. Their own ITA filings report a median of 60 employees, with only 8.6% under 20, and 14–18% of them have five or more inspections, against 3.6% overall. Establishments with a median headcount under 20 make up **85.4% of the population, but only 6.2% of them can carry a label**, against 28.7% of larger ones. Person-named, sole-proprietor-like establishments are 9.6% of the population, with 1.3% eligible. The ten largest EINs supply 42% of positive pairs, D.R. Horton alone 10.4%. Five firms supply a third of the sampled positives, and only 15.4% of construction inspections belong to label-eligible establishments. The metrics therefore describe matching for national homebuilders, large GCs and elevator companies, not the small firm with one or two OSHA records that a GC typically vets.

The EIN behaves as a legal-entity identifier, not a family one. In construction ITA filings, 91% of EINs carry a single cleaned name and only 1.7% of establishments change EIN between years. Yet families file under many EINs: TurnPoint Services under 48, D.R. Horton under 31 and Walsh Group under 23 in a single state. 17.6% of names with two or more establishments carry more than one EIN ([local data checks](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/local_data_checks.md)). Academic work linking injury data to firms hit the same wall: one firm holds several EINs, and its establishments report different ones ([Cohn, Nestoriak & Wardlaw](https://faculty.mccombs.utexas.edu/jonathan.cohn/papers/PE_buyouts_and_workplace_safety_2020dec.pdf)). EIN equality is therefore strong evidence of a match, and EIN inequality only weak evidence against one. Two data-quality fixes ride along. EIN is essentially absent before 2019. And one EIN covering 723 "FACILITY n" establishments slips past the pipeline's junk rule, because all its names start with the same word.

Five evaluation changes follow, in order. The first is a four-level label (same establishment, same legal entity, same family, unrelated). Re-mark the strong-family negatives as related, and report legal-entity and family precision separately. FlexER formalises this as one pair carrying different labels under different resolution intents ([FlexER](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07569)). The second caps pairs per EIN and stratifies every metric by size band, inspection count, person-name flag, and same versus different name. The 2026 CorpFam benchmark showed why: an aggregate F1 of 76.8 hid near-zero recall on its hardest stratum ([CorpFam](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04269v1)).

The third is a query-level gold set. Sample GC-style inputs, either real entries or OSHA names perturbed the way GCs type them. Label every candidate a deliberately loose blocker returns. Then estimate precision and recall with design-weighted estimators, because benchmark-style precision and recall in entity resolution are biased and over-optimistic ([Binette et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01230)). The Census Bureau's MAMBA linker was trained on about 2,000 clerically reviewed records, sampled evenly across score bands ([Cuffe & Goldschlag](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2018/CES-WP-18-46.pdf)). An LLM can pre-label for a person to confirm. Labelling a benchmark training set with GPT-5.2 cost $28–41, against an estimated 470 hours by hand, and models trained on those labels came within 2 F1 points of models trained on human labels ([Steiner & Bizer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28823)).

The fourth draws small-firm labels from identifiers the project can reach without ITA: WA UBIs confirmed through L&I inspection numbers, and licence numbers. The fifth re-tunes the adjudicator thresholds on the new set and adds temporal and state hold-outs. Jev's 0.85 match cut-off and its 0.20 and 0.06 exclusion cut-offs were picked on seed 7 of these same silver pairs ([jev.py](../ssi/llm/jev.py); [adjudication eval](../eval/adjudication/run.py)).

For context, published OSHA linkages report only match rates and no held-out precision. Lee and Taylor's Census linkage matched about 60% before clerical review, and Johnson, Levine and Toffel linked 82% of targeted establishments to at least one inspection ([Lee & Taylor appendix](https://topcat.aeaweb.org/articles/materials/11737); [Johnson, Levine & Toffel](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/improving%20regulatory%20effectiveness--final%20working%20paper_dbebc460-588b-4eb4-bf97-0f683bac5f70.pdf)). The project's evaluation is already more rigorous than the literature; it only needs pointing at the right population.

## A quarter-sized California licence file tops the owned-data fixes

California is the project's biggest coverage hole and the easiest to fill. The CSLB file the warehouse was built from has **45,786 lines and ends mid-record**, and the pipeline's own comment calls it a partial download ([cslb_master.csv](../data/raw/reference/ca_cslb/cslb_master.csv); [61_licence.sql](../ssi/pipeline/sql/61_licence.sql)). The full License Master is **57,079,888 bytes and 179,087 rows**, and it is free. It is served after a two-step ASP.NET postback as a chunked stream with no Content-Length, and the download takes over two minutes. The truncation is a client timeout. The fix is to keep the session cookie, stream to disk with no timeout and check the row count ([CSLB data portal](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/onlineservices/dataportal/ContractorList)). The gap lines up with California's licence-link rate. Only **13% of California OSHA construction establishments inspected since 2020 (1,210 of 9,404) link to a licence, against 57% in Washington and 63% in Oregon**, and California's pool is the second largest after Texas among the ten states probed ([coverage probes](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/source_coverage_probes.md)). The same portal's Personnel file names the officers and responsible managing officers or employees behind each licence, which ties one person to several entities. The master records business type (for example "Sole Owner") and workers'-comp policy numbers, which link entities independently of names. Each file needs its own dropdown postback. Licences cancelled or expired for more than five years drop out, so firms renamed early in the window will still be missing.

Washington shows a second cheap win, in the linking rules rather than the data. M1 links a licence to an OSHA establishment only on the same cleaned name plus zip, and M2 only on the same cleaned name plus address key ([62_ref_link.sql](../ssi/pipeline/sql/62_ref_link.sql)). When the Washington prevailing-wage probe found 6 of 14 unlinked sample firms by name, every one was already in the licence file and had been missed ([coverage probes](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/source_coverage_probes.md); [WA intents API](https://data.wa.gov/resource/t9je-9qwa.json)). OSHA still records Royal Roofing & Siding, the name the firm filed under until 2015, while the licence file holds only its new name, Royal Roofing Inc, at the same street and UBI. Style Corp holds its licence under the trade name SERVPRO OF SHORELINE/WOODINVLL at the same address. Valley Electric Co. of Mt. Vernon appears in the licence file as VALLEY ELEC CO/MT VERNON INC. And Evergreen Concrete Cutting, Pennon Construction and Jr G Concrete Design carry licence addresses in different zips from their OSHA records.

Three rule changes address these. First, let a distinctive name link within the state without zip agreement, and send the link to UNCERTAIN rather than dropping it. Second, expand common abbreviations such as ELEC, CONST and SVS before comparing. Third, treat every name a UBI has filed or been licensed under as an alias. The WA principal file, 250,718 rows of principals with start and end dates per UBI, is downloaded but never loaded. The licence table also drops the phone number and primary principal the source carries ([local data checks](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/local_data_checks.md); [WA principal data](https://data.wa.gov/d/4xk5-x9j6)).

Washington also hands the project an identifier hidden in plain sight. Since 2015, almost every WA-site inspection name has started with the L&I inspection number: WA plus nine digits. **21,690 warehouse inspections** carry one, but only inside the raw name, while 10,302 establishment display names still show the number to GCs ([local data checks](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/local_data_checks.md)). The number is unique per inspection, so it never groups an employer on its own. But L&I's citation-document endpoint returns the citation only for the correct UBI and inspection-number pair, and the citation shows legal name, DBA, UBI and both addresses. The number alone, or with a wrong UBI, falls back to a search page ([correct pair](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/Details/CitationDocument.aspx?LIC=&UBI=603106218&SAW=&VIO=&InspectionNo=317962473); [number alone](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/Details/CitationDocument.aspx?InspectionNo=317962473)). That makes the endpoint a confirmation test. Propose UBIs by name and address from the licence table the project already holds; one GET per candidate then settles each one. That includes joint ventures such as the Flatiron/Lane JV that name matching missed. The prize is **5,049 WA establishments with no licence link, covering 8,995 inspections, of which 5,513 carry citations**.

The evidence for this route is thin. It rests on one correct and one mismatched pair, and it works only where a citation exists. L&I's terms of use and rate limits have not been reviewed. The per-UBI inspection list on the Verify page is filled by JavaScript from an endpoint nobody has inspected ([Verify detail page](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/Detail.aspx?UBI=603106218&LIC=ALLWARI891KO&SAW=)). A public-records request for an inspection-to-UBI extract is the cleaner bulk route. Oregon's nine-digit numbers, on 11,030 warehouse rows, invite the same test.

Inside OSHA's own data, the leads are exhausted or long shots. Beyond names, both addresses, industry codes and headcount, the inspection file carries no identity, and the 12 columns the warehouse drops are empty, constant or placeholders in the window ([local data checks](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/local_data_checks.md)). The one untested public table is `related_activity`. It pairs each inspection with related activity numbers under type codes such as C and R, whose meaning is unverified, and it downloads from the DOL catalogue the project already uses ([labordata mirror](https://labordata.bunkum.us/osha_enforcement/related_activity); [DOL catalogue](https://apiprod.dol.gov/v4/datasets)). OSHA has encouraged inspectors to collect EINs since 2019, but those EINs are not in the public file ([OSHA memo](https://www.osha.gov/memos/2020-12-22/employer-identification-number)). Researchers have obtained non-public OSHA identifiers only under a memorandum of understanding ([Johnson, Levine & Toffel](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/improving%20regulatory%20effectiveness--final%20working%20paper_dbebc460-588b-4eb4-bf97-0f683bac5f70.pdf)), so a FOIA or MOU request is worth one letter, not a plan. GAO hit the same wall in 1996. It verified matches by telephone and recovered prior inspection histories for only about half of worksites, and construction was the hardest sector ([GAO/HEHS-96-157](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-HEHS-96-157/html/GAOREPORTS-HEHS-96-157.htm)).

"Verified" means the name agrees and a street, zip or city agrees with an OSHA mailing address. "Probable" means a distinctive name matches in the same state at a different address. The results come from live queries against the [NY DOS](https://data.ny.gov/d/n9v6-gdp6), [Texas franchise](https://data.texas.gov/d/9cir-efmm) and [sales-tax](https://data.texas.gov/d/jrea-zgmq), [WA intents](https://data.wa.gov/resource/t9je-9qwa.json), [USAspending](https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/recipient/) and [FMCSA](https://data.transportation.gov/resource/az4n-8mr2.json) endpoints, judged by hand ([coverage probes](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/source_coverage_probes.md)).

Three patterns matter more than the ranking ([coverage probes](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/source_coverage_probes.md)). First, most hits confirm rather than fix. 17 of 20 FMCSA hits, and all but one USAspending hit, were exact matches after the project's own cleaning. Only a minority exposed a second name: 5 of 26 in New York (three renames from the name-history table, two DBAs from the DOL registry), 4 of 18 in Washington, 4 of 20 in FMCSA, 2 of 18 in Texas and none in USAspending. Those second names are the payload for the hard failure modes. Woodcock & Armani, for instance, exists in New York only as a DBA of Comfort Systems USA (Syracuse), and Ferguson Electric Construction Co. became Ferguson Electric Inc. in 2022 ([NY DOL registry](https://data.ny.gov/d/i4jv-zkey); [NY name history](https://data.ny.gov/d/ekwr-p59j)).

Second, coverage tracks why each source exists. FMCSA verifies 40% of large firms but 13% of small ones, because it follows truck ownership. USAspending's apparent breadth is the 2020–21 PPP borrower list: 14 of its 21 verified hits rested on records without a UEI, 13 of them SBA loans. SBA's bulk PPP files would deliver those more cheaply than the API's two calls per establishment ([PPP FOIA](https://data.sba.gov/dataset/ppp-foia)). Third, **sole proprietors defeat every source**. Of 14 person-named establishments across the samples, one was found: a Los Angeles contractor registered with FMCSA under the owner's name plus CONSTRUCTION, with a separate DBA. Common names pulled in namesakes from other cities.

Washington's prevailing-wage filings earn their rank for relationships rather than coverage. The Intents table (1.33 million rows, updated daily) names every contractor at every tier of a public-works project. Each row carries the contractor's UBI, licence and industrial-insurance account, plus the hiring company's UBI ([WA intents metadata](https://data.wa.gov/api/views/t9je-9qwa.json)). That yields GC-to-sub edges with identifiers at both ends; Puget Paving alone appears under 234 hiring companies. It also yields a site key. Of ten multi-employer OSHA site groups tested, the filings reproduced one whole three-tier chain at Northgate Elementary, where Lydig Construction hired Inland Steel Erectors, which hired Ford Crane. They matched the GC alone at a second site and found nothing at the other eight, mostly private developments ([coverage probes](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/source_coverage_probes.md)).

### Untested free files target DBAs, renames and families directly

Four free national files pair a trade name with a legal name or an EIN, and none has been probed against OSHA employers. WHD's enforcement file lists each concluded case's trade name and legal name side by side, with street, city, zip and NAICS, since FY2005. It is a 200 MB zip on the data.dol.gov URL pattern the project already uses, refreshed quarterly, with no key ([WHD zip](https://data.dol.gov/data-catalog/WHD/enforcement/WHD_enforcement.zip)). labordata's dedupe gazetteer already joins it to OSHA by name and address, with no published evaluation ([labordata/employer-links](https://github.com/labordata/employer-links)).

Form 5500-SF carries the sponsor's name, DBA, EIN and NAICS, and a physical location separate from the mailing address. It also carries the last-reported sponsor name and EIN, a direct rename signal. Files cover 2009–2025, refresh monthly, need no account, and join straight to the ITA EINs the project holds ([EBSA datasets](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/public-disclosure/foia/form-5500-datasets); [5500-SF 2024](https://askebsa.dol.gov/FOIA%20Files/2024/Latest/F_5500_SF_2024_Latest.zip)). OFLC's H-2B disclosure files give legal name, DBA and FEIN for construction and landscaping employers. FEIN is in the FY2025 layout but appears to be excluded in some earlier years ([H-2B layout FY2025 Q4](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/ETA/oflc/pdfs/H-2B_Record_Layout_FY2025_Q4.pdf); [FY2024 Q1](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/ETA/oflc/pdfs/H-2B_Record_Layout_FY2024_Q1.pdf)). SEC EDGAR fixes the city-as-division problem outright for public builders. D.R. Horton's FY2025 Exhibit 21 lists 617 entities, including "D.R. Horton, Inc. - Greensboro" and separate city divisions from Birmingham to Portland, with a DBA column filled on 178 rows. Each registrant's submissions file gives its dated former names ([DHI Exhibit 21](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/882184/000088218425000081/a9302025ex211subsidiaries.htm); [EDGAR APIs](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces)).

Federal award data matters less. SAM.gov's monthly public extract needs only a free account and API key, not a paid one. It carries UEI, legal name, DBA, an entity division name, and physical and mailing addresses, but no parent fields. The public API's documented ownership and predecessor section is untested ([SAM extracts](https://open.gsa.gov/api/sam-entity-extracts-api/); [Entity API](https://open.gsa.gov/api/entity-api/)). It covers only firms that sought federal awards: just 12% of the multi-state sample had a verified UEI-bearing record, all of them large ([coverage probes](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/source_coverage_probes.md)). USAspending's subaward files are the only free national record naming a sub's prime contractor, but they reached 5% of the sample. The ITA case-detail files from 2023 onward add the owning company's name beside each establishment name and EIN, which helps cluster families among filers ([OSHA ITA data](https://www.osha.gov/Establishment-Specific-Injury-and-Illness-Data)).

Beyond New York and Texas, the state picture splits into free and paid. Florida, with 6,569 construction establishments inspected since 2020, publishes its whole corporate registry free by SFTP. Each record carries the FEI number and up to six officers, and there is a separate fictitious-name file ([Sunbiz file definitions](https://dos.sunbiz.org/data-definitions/cor.html)). Florida's licence board's extracts carry a doing-business-as field but block non-US IP addresses ([DBPR public records](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/construction-industry/public-records/)). Its workers'-comp portal exports construction exemptions searchable by FEIN, naming the officers of exempt firms ([FL DFS exemptions](https://dwcdataportal.fldfs.com/Exemption.aspx)).

Colorado and Connecticut publish name-change tables (58,151 and 96,682 changes) alongside New York's ([CO history](https://data.colorado.gov/d/casm-dbbj); [CT name changes](https://data.ct.gov/d/enwv-52we)). Oregon's registry includes 234,232 assumed business names linked to their registrants, and its workers'-comp employer list pairs legal names with DBAs and NAICS ([OR active businesses](https://data.oregon.gov/d/tckn-sxa6); [OR WC employers](https://data.oregon.gov/d/q9zj-c8r2)). Pennsylvania, Iowa and Alaska publish free entity files, Alaska's with an assumed-name column ([PA](https://data.pa.gov/d/xvd7-5r2c); [IA](https://data.iowa.gov/catalog/dataset/554); [AK](https://www.commerce.alaska.gov/cbp/main/DbDownload/CorporationsDownload)). Free licence lists that pair a business with a person or a DBA exist in Minnesota, Virginia, Texas and New York City ([MN DLI](https://www.dli.mn.gov/business/construction-contractor-registration); [VA DPOR](https://www.dpor.virginia.gov/RegulantLists); [TDLR](https://data.texas.gov/d/7358-krk7); [NYC DOB](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/d/t8hj-ruu2)).

The Southeast mostly charges. Tennessee asks $1,000 for a database that includes assumed names, Georgia $1,000 or $500 a month, and North Carolina $750 plus $2,000 a year. Alabama's Secretary of State offers no bulk file at all, which leaves the general-contractor board's CSV list as the state's only structured source ([TN](https://tnbear.tn.gov/Ecommerce/DBDownloadWizard.aspx); [GA](https://georgia.gov/bulk-corporations-data); [NC](https://b2b.sosnc.gov/fees/by_title/_data_subscriptions); [AL SOS](https://www.sos.alabama.gov/business-entities/business-downloads); [AL board](https://licensesearch.alabama.gov/genconbd)). Texas's $1,750 SOS master adds little, because the free Comptroller files cover active entities with their SOS file numbers ([TX SOS bulk](https://direct.sos.state.tx.us/help/help-corp.asp?pg=bulk)).

## Paid data suits per-sub lookups and family trees, not bulk backfill

Paid sources split by what they price. Dun & Bradstreet is the only source with site-level family trees for private firms. A third-party summary of a 2024 federal schedule puts it at about $0.24 per identity match and $19.70 per full family tree. But D&B licenses per use case and restricts display, which matters for a product that shows results to GCs ([SavvyIQ on D&B](https://savvyiq.ai/compare/dun-bradstreet)). BrightQuery keys organisations on EIN, LEI and CIK, so it would join directly to the project's ITA EINs; pricing is by contact, and its free tier is non-commercial ([BrightQuery docs](https://docs.brightquery.com/business_identity_api); [OpenSanctions listing](https://www.opensanctions.org/datasets/brightquery/)). NETS, the D&B-derived research standard, runs $50,625–$800,000 per federal purchase order under research licences ([GovTribe](https://govtribe.com/opportunity/federal-contract-opportunity/purchase-of-nets-national-establishment-time-series-data-1232sa24p0008)).

At the per-lookup end, Enigma models trade names and legal entities, and represents sole proprietors as people. It charges $1.50 for a KYB identify call and $2.50 for verify, and claims 95% precision on brand-to-legal-entity links ([Enigma pricing](https://www.enigma.com/pricing); [Enigma docs](https://documentation.enigma.com/getting_started/enigma_data)). Google Places Text Search costs $32 per 1,000 calls after 5,000 free a month, but only the place ID may be stored ([Google pricing](https://developers.google.com/maps/billing-and-pricing/pricing); [Places policies](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/places/web-service/policies)). OpenCorporates has the right data model, with typed previous and trading names across all 50 states. But its self-serve plans sell 500–5,000 calls a month for £2,250–£12,000 a year, which cannot backfill hundreds of thousands of OSHA names ([OpenCorporates pricing](https://opencorporates.com/pricing/)). Overture's free, commercially usable layer of about 81 million places worldwide would be the free existence check, but it went unprobed, and contractors without storefronts are its weak spot ([Overture places](https://docs.overturemaps.org/guides/places/)).

Two paid sources serve evaluation and families more than matching. Violation Tracker links OSHA penalties of $5,000 or more to over 3,000 large parents, with manual checks, and shows both the parent at the time of the penalty and the current one. Subscriptions run $250–$1,500 a year, and the full dataset is licensed to academics and about a dozen companies ([user guide](https://violationtracker.goodjobsfirst.org/pages/user-guide); [plans](https://violationtracker.goodjobsfirst.org/plans); [licensing note](https://goodjobsfirst.org/message-to-violation-tracker-users/)). Its links are ready-made labels for testing a RELATED tier on big families, though they say nothing about small subs. Shovels sells permit-derived contractor records from $599 a month, with a DBA field and state-level contractor groups that consolidate related entities. Yet it matched only about 200,000 of its 1.8 million licensed contractors to permits, a reminder that permits miss most small subs ([Shovels pricing](https://www.shovels.ai/pricing); [Shovels on grouping](https://www.shovels.ai/blog/construction-leads-permit-data/); [licence file](https://www.shovels.ai/blog/introducing-the-shovels-nationwide-contractor-state-license-file/)).

The cheapest identifiers for a GC's own subs are the ones the GC already holds. Procore's company directory stores each vendor's state licence number and trade. Autodesk's TradeTapp API exposes prequalification questionnaires and financials for loading into a GC's warehouse ([Procore import guide](https://support.procore.com/products/procore-imports/company-directory/tutorials/prepare-users-and-vendors-for-import-to-the-procore-imports-app); [TradeTapp API](https://aps.autodesk.com/en/docs/buildingconnected/v2/developers_guide/field_guide/tradetapp/)). The project's company-profile step already spends a web search on each sub. As built, it reports the published name, website, a summary and quoted locations, but not the other names a company trades under or the licence numbers on its site ([profile.py](../ssi/llm/profile.py)). Adding those fields under the same quote-checking rule is the near-free version of a KYB call. Before buying anything, run a 200-name pilot through D&B, Enigma, BrightQuery and OpenCorporates, weighted toward small, person-named and DBA cases, because no vendor has published recall on OSHA employer names.

## Method changes must widen candidates before sharpening scores

The CorpFam benchmark explains why string similarity alone cannot fix families or DBAs. Name-based methods recovered every identical-name family pair and 64.3% of pairs sharing a distinctive token, but under 5% of pairs sharing none. 93.2% of those never entered any candidate set, and the union of seven blocking schemes reached only 6.8% ([CorpFam](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04269v1)). The project's own 29-of-65 miss rate on different-name pairs says the same.

The first method change is therefore candidate routes driven by identifiers and alias tables. Join on EIN, UBI and licence number wherever the sub or a reference file supplies one. Look the sub's name up in an alias table assembled from licence DBAs and principals, registry name histories and assumed names, WHD trade-and-legal pairs, Form 5500 sponsor and prior names, FMCSA legal and DBA names, and Exhibit 21 divisions. Alongside those, add a character 3-gram TF-IDF top-k route, which ING's sparse_dot_topn makes cheap ([ING](https://medium.com/wbaa/even-faster-string-comparison-2778be7fe480)), an initials key that counts only with zip or address agreement, and DBA splitting on D/B/A and T/A. Then measure each route's marginal recall per stratum.

The second change gives corporate families their own outcome. A RELATED status beside MATCHED, UNCERTAIN and EXCLUDED lets the product show a family's history separately without merging it into the sub's. Commercial engines follow the same pattern, reporting relationships as well as matches ([Senzing](https://senzing.com/explore-senzing-entity-resolution/)). The visible-family signature is detectable in SQL: a rare distinctive core agrees, the names differ only in place, division or descriptor words, and the EINs or mailing metros differ. When the place word equals the sub's entered city, the record is the right division and should match that division only; otherwise it is RELATED. That one rule turns the D.R. Horton contradictions and most of the 17 family "false positives" into correct outcomes, and Exhibit 21 or Violation Tracker supply labels to check it.

The third change replaces hand-set thresholds with learned weights per evidence level. That is where 37 of the 42 uncertain true matches sit, and the change is safe only once the RELATED tier absorbs same-name family branches. Token classes (legal suffix, generic descriptor, trade word, place word, person token, distinctive token), with IDF computed over establishments, make HAGERMAN versus HAGERMAN CONSTRUCTION nearly free and SMITH ROOFING versus JONES ROOFING expensive. That is the soft TF-IDF design that beat other name metrics in Cohen and colleagues' comparison ([Cohen et al. 2003](https://pubs.dbs.uni-leipzig.de/dc/files/Cohen2003Acomparisonofstringdistance.pdf)). Splink is MIT-licensed and runs on DuckDB by default. Its term-frequency adjustments are the principled form of the project's distinctiveness statistic. It learns m-probabilities from labelled pairs such as the EIN positives, and it runs query-time matching against new records ([Splink](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink); [TF guide](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/term-frequency.html); [training API](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/api_docs/training.html)). The GC's optional trade fits in as a compatible, conflicting or unknown level, fed by NAICS from OSHA, licence classifications and the new files.

Three cautions apply. Compute term frequencies over establishments rather than inspections, so a firm inspected 400 times does not look common. Model the place word and the city as one combined level, because Fellegi–Sunter assumes independent evidence. And never lean on the name column alone, which Splink's own README warns against. On Census business records, moving from two aggregate scores to per-comparator weights lifted F1 from 0.858 to 0.920, and a random forest reached about 0.95 ([Cuffe & Goldschlag](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2018/CES-WP-18-46.pdf)). Census also found that strict address matching mattered more than strict name matching ([Kim & McCue](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cen/wpaper/20-03.html)). Addresses need frequency weights of their own, because job sites, registered agents and mail drops are shared; one Georgia suite returned eight unrelated motor carriers ([coverage probes](../research_notes/OSHA%20subcontractor%20matching%20data%20sources/source_coverage_probes.md)). So compare the GC's city with OSHA mailing fields rather than job-site fields. Standardise addresses with usaddress, and geocode them free through the Census Geocoder at up to 10,000 addresses per batch ([Census Geocoder FAQ](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/maps-data/data/Census_Geocoder_FAQ.pdf); [usaddress](https://pypi.org/project/usaddress/0.2.6)).

The fourth change is how the adjudicators see a sub. Today each uncertain cluster goes in its own call, to Jev (TypeSafe's decision model) or, when red-flagged, to the LLM ([adjudicator.py](../ssi/llm/adjudicator.py)). A list-wise "select" prompt, showing one query with all its candidates, beat pairwise matching by 81.6% to 64.0% F1 across eight benchmarks, at $1.71 against $4.52 ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16884)). Accuracy fell when the true match sat late in the list, so shuffle the order, and run red-flagged subs twice in reversed order. Seeing a sub's clusters together also enforces consistency, since a sub matched to both A and B implies that A and B are one firm. Calibrate whatever produces P(same) on the new gold set; temperature scaling cut a matcher's calibration error by up to 24% across four datasets, one of them of companies ([Kamsteeg et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19557)). An in-house cross-encoder trained on EIN pairs plus LLM-teacher labels is the literature's most cost-efficient scorer: cross-encoders beat bi-encoders, and larger models lean more on shortcuts ([Zhang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24688)). But Jev is cheap and already tuned, so a cross-encoder is worth building only if Jev's unannounced version changes start moving results ([jev.py](../ssi/llm/jev.py)). The rule that sends fatality, willful, repeat and failure-to-abate clusters to the GC regardless should stay.

## Conclusion

The evidence moves the problem. The headline precision reflects a product decision the system has not yet made: is a division of the sub's parent the sub? Once a RELATED tier encodes the answer, precision against unrelated companies is already near 0.99 on this sample. What remains is a recall deficit on the cases the silver labels barely contain: different names, small firms and people's names. Those cases yield to identifiers far more than to similarity, because their alternate names usually share no distinctive token with the OSHA record and so never reach the scorer. That makes the licence number the product's most valuable input field, and every complete licence or registry file a multiplier on it. Prompting for a licence number when a sub's name looks like a person's, backed by full licence files for each state the GCs work in, will do more for sole proprietors than any database the research tested.

The remaining work sorts by geography rather than by technique. New York, Texas and Washington can be covered free, with measured coverage of 37–87%; Florida can be covered free, with coverage still to measure. Much of the Southeast costs $1,000 or more per state, and Alabama's registry offers no bulk file at all. Ingestion should therefore follow the GCs' footprint, not a national plan. Paid data earns a place only for private corporate families and per-sub confirmation, and only after a pilot measures the recall on OSHA names that no vendor has published.
