# Entity-resolution / record-linkage methods for matching GC-entered subcontractors to OSHA construction inspection records

Scope note: researched 2026-10-04. Publication years are given for every paper. "Project" = the site-safety-intelligence pipeline: deterministic DuckDB cleaning, then exact-key establishments, then multi-route candidate generation, then ordered rules (MATCHED / UNCERTAIN / EXCLUDED), then the Jev/LLM adjudicator, evaluated against EIN-based ITA silver labels. Current numbers: precision 0.85, candidate recall 0.95, 28% of true positives left uncertain, 1.3% false exclusion. Everything under "Inferences" is my reasoning or recommendation, not a sourced fact.

## 1. Probabilistic record linkage tools (Fellegi–Sunter and others): which suits a DuckDB/Python team, and how does it relate to the project's "distinctiveness" stat and silver labels?

### Takeaway
Splink (UK Ministry of Justice, MIT licence, DuckDB as default backend) fits this stack best. Its term-frequency (TF) adjustments are the principled form of the project's "distinctiveness" statistic. Its m-probabilities can be estimated directly from the EIN silver pairs, and `find_matches_to_new_records` already does the per-subcontractor, query-time matching this product runs. Its own README warns that it is not built for a lone company-name column, so it pays off only if city/state/zip/address/trade are modelled as separate comparisons. The other tools are a poor fit or worth borrowing only for ideas: fastLink is in R, dedupe and Zingg are built around active learning, Senzing is proprietary with a capped free tier, and ING's EMM is the closest company-name-specific library.

### Cited Findings
**Splink: core facts**
- Splink is an MIT-licensed Python package. Its backends are DuckDB (the default, runs on a laptop), Spark (for 100M+ records) and PostgreSQL. The README claims it links about 1 million records in roughly 60 seconds on a laptop with DuckDB, and says "Splink 5 was recently released". ONS used it for 2021 Census linkage. — [Splink GitHub README](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink)
- Direct quote from the README: "Splink is not designed for linking a single column containing a 'bag of words'. For example, a table with a single 'company name' column, and no other details." — [Splink GitHub README](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink)
- Splink performs best with several input columns that are not highly correlated. For companies, the docs suggest columns such as name, turnover, sector and telephone number. — [Splink docs / search summary](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/index.html)
- Splink has been used for companies. The UK Competition and Markets Authority used it to identify "Persons with Significant Control" and estimated ownership groups across companies. The Department for Business and Trade planned to use it in "Matchbox" to reconcile business and product data. — [Real World Data Science case study (2023)](https://realworlddatascience.net/applied-insights/case-studies/posts/2023/11/22/splink.html)
- Statistics Canada's 2022 symposium paper is a case study of using Splink to find duplicate census responses. — [StatCan 11-522-x (2022)](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11-522-x/2022001/article/00002-eng.pdf)

**Splink term-frequency adjustments (compared with the project's distinctiveness stat)**
- The TF adjustment is a separate term added to the match weight. Common values (e.g. "Smith") get lower weight and rare values get higher weight. "The TF adjustment doesn't depend on m, and therefore does not have to be estimated by the EM algorithm - it is known already." The adjustment works through the u-probability (the chance two non-matching records agree), and "The EM algorithm benefits from the TF adjustment (rather than previous post hoc implementations)." — [Splink TF topic guide](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/term-frequency.html)
- TF adjustments also apply at fuzzy comparison levels, where Splink "will simply use the greater of the two term frequencies". `tf_adjustment_weight` scales the effect (0 = off, 0.5 = halved). `tf_minimum_u_value` puts a floor under very rare values, such as misspellings, so they cannot produce outsized weights (e.g. any frequency below 1 in 1000 is set to 0.001). — [Splink TF topic guide](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/term-frequency.html)
- `register_term_frequency_lookup(input_data, col_name)` lets you supply a precomputed TF table for a column instead of having Splink compute one from the input. It is intended for cases where the full population is larger than the records being linked. — [Splink Table Management API](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/api_docs/table_management.html); feature request background in [Splink issue #1992](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink/issues/1992)
- A community-documented method combines array-valued fields, such as company names split into tokens, with TF adjustments. — [Splink Discussion #2022](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink/discussions/2022)

**Splink training with labels (silver EIN pairs)**
- `linker.training` provides `estimate_u_using_random_sampling()`, `estimate_parameters_using_expectation_maximisation()` (by default estimates m), `estimate_m_from_pairwise_labels()` (takes a table of labelled matching pairs), `estimate_m_from_label_column()` (takes a ground-truth ID column, even if only partly populated) and `estimate_probability_two_random_records_match()`. — [Splink training API](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/api_docs/training.html)

**Splink query-time / incremental matching**
- `linker.find_matches_to_new_records` compares a few new records against the existing dataset and returns candidates ranked by Splink score. It is fast because only new-vs-existing pairs are generated. Fully incremental linkage was described as not yet easily supported. — [Splink real-time linkage demo (DuckDB)](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/demos/examples/duckdb/real_time_record_linkage.html); [IJPDS abstract "Splink: recent developments and realtime linkage"](https://ijpds.org/index.php/ijpds/article/view/3598); [Splink inference API](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/api_docs/inference.html)

**fastLink (R)**
- Enamorado, Fifield & Imai, "Using a Probabilistic Model to Assist Merging of Large-Scale Administrative Records", *APSR* 113(2):353–371 (2019). It is a fast, scalable Fellegi–Sunter implementation fitted by EM that handles missing data and can include auxiliary information. It ships as the R package fastLink. — [APSR paper PDF](https://imai.fas.harvard.edu/research/files/linkage.pdf); [fastLink GitHub](https://github.com/kosukeimai/fastLink)

**dedupe, Zingg, recordlinkage, pyJedAI, Senzing, ING EMM**
- dedupe (Python) popularised the "label some pairs, let the model learn the rest" active-learning approach. It is well documented and suited to small and medium datasets rather than billion-row jobs. Zingg (Python/Java) is also active-learning based and runs natively on Databricks, Snowflake, Fabric, EMR and Glue. The Python Record Linkage Toolkit is described as suited to modular linkage prototyping. Note that this source is a vendor blog. — [Tilores blog (2026)](https://tilores.io/content/best-open-source-entity-resolution-and-record-linkage-libraries-splink-zingg-dedupe-and-when-to-move-beyond-them); [Zingg "Top 10 open source ER tools 2026"](https://www.zingg.ai/post/top-10-open-source-entity-resolution-tools-2026) (also a vendor; it lists Zingg, Splink, dedupe, Python Record Linkage Toolkit, PyJedAI, fastLink, dblink, R RecordLinkage, DeepMatcher, RELAIS); curated list at [Awesome-Entity-Resolution](https://github.com/OlivierBinette/Awesome-Entity-Resolution)
- Senzing's free Desktop Evaluation tool supports up to 100,000 records; larger free evaluations are available on request. The engine combines deterministic rules with probabilistic scoring and handles spelling variation, transposed identifier digits and partial address overlap. The production SDK scales to billions of records. — [Senzing desktop trial](https://senzing.com/desktop/); [Senzing get-started](https://senzing.com/explore-senzing-entity-resolution/)
- ING's Entity Matching Model (EMM, MIT licence, `pip install emm`, pandas and Spark implementations) is built specifically for company-name matching. Three candidate indexers work together: word-level TF-IDF cosine, character n-gram TF-IDF cosine (sped up with `sparse_dot_topn`) and sorted-neighbourhood indexing. A supervised classifier then scores candidates using string-, rank- and legal-entity-based features. "Name aggregation" combines scores when several names belong to one account. — [ING EMM GitHub](https://github.com/ing-bank/EntityMatchingModel); [EMM docs](https://entitymatchingmodel.readthedocs.io/en/latest/); [ING blog "Matching company names at scale"](https://medium.com/wbaa/matching-company-names-at-scale-af20429a80c7)

**Census Bureau business-record linkage (closest public analogue)**
- Census Bureau MAMBA (Cuffe & Goldschlag, CES WP 18-46, 2018) links external business records to the Business Register. It standardises names and addresses (abbreviations, suffixes) and blocks geographically: 5-digit zip, then city, then 3-digit zip, then state, then a residual pass against the whole register that requires at least one shared Soundex word code. Residual blocks keep only pairs with average score above 0.5. Thirteen FEBRL string comparators on name and address feed a random forest trained on about 2,000 clerically reviewed records, stratified into score bands 0.5–0.7, 0.7–0.9 and 0.9–1 so rare matches are sampled. On 1,900 pairs, an additive logit on aggregate name and address scores reached F1 0.858 (P 0.877, R 0.846). A logit on all individual comparators reached F1 0.920 (P 0.915, R 0.926, AUC 0.970). The random forest reached cross-validated precision above 95% (P/R/F1 about 95, AUC about 98). — [Cuffe & Goldschlag, CES-WP-18-46 (2018)](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2018/CES-WP-18-46.pdf)
- Kim & McCue (Census CES WP 20-03, 2020) matched Massachusetts and California state business-registration records to Census data, with match rates of 45% (MA) and 40% (CA). They found that "using relatively strict matching on address is important for match accuracy, while results are less sensitive to name matching strictness." MAMBA's machine-learning method produced more matches, but SAS DQ was more accurate. — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cen/wpaper/20-03.html)
- Winkler (Census, 1993) describes splitting establishment names into components (first name, initial, surname, business keyword) to improve matching. — [Winkler, "Matching and Record Linkage" (1993)](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/1993/adrm/rr93-8.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Distinctiveness compared with Splink TF.** The project's distinctiveness counts *types*: how many distinct full names share a name core. Splink's default TF counts *rows*: how often a value appears in the input table. Run on raw OSHA inspection rows, row-based TF would be wrong here. A firm inspected 400 times would look "common" even though it is one entity, which is the familiar dedupe-within-one-dataset trap. **Recommendation:** compute TF tables over the establishments table, or over distinct (cleaned name, state), so a value's frequency is approximately "number of distinct entities carrying this value". Register them with `register_term_frequency_lookup`. The project's distinctiveness count is already close to this and can be reused as the TF lookup for a "name core" column.
- Splink's `tf_minimum_u_value` floor and its "use the greater TF for fuzzy levels" rule are the probabilistic counterparts of the project's dominance-count typo correction. Both stop a rare misspelling of a common name from looking highly distinctive. This is evidence that the existing typo step is sound, and the floor value can be tuned on silver labels rather than hand-set.
- **How Splink would fit.** Do not replace the pipeline. Use Splink, or a hand-built Fellegi–Sunter scorer in DuckDB SQL, as the scoring layer *between* candidate generation and the rules. Model each existing rule signal as a comparison with levels, for example:
  - name: exact full name / same core + descriptor-only difference / same core + place-word difference / JW ≥ 0.92 on core / else;
  - person-name flag;
  - city: exact / same county or MSA / different;
  - state;
  - zip5;
  - address: exact standardised / same street / different;
  - trade: compatible / conflict / unknown.

  Estimate m from EIN-positive pairs with `estimate_m_from_pairwise_labels`, u from random sampling, and TF from establishment-level tables. The output is a summed log-Bayes-factor with waterfall-chart explanations. Thresholds on it can then replace the ordered-rule cascade's MATCHED/UNCERTAIN cut-offs. Splink's documented constraint (do not rely on name alone) maps directly onto the existing "city/state/address agreement" logic.
- **Expected gain:** mostly a *smaller UNCERTAIN bucket* (28% of true positives now) and better-calibrated scores, rather than higher candidate recall. MAMBA's jump from two aggregated scores (F1 0.86) to per-comparator features (0.92 logit, about 0.95 RF) suggests that replacing a few hand-tuned thresholds with learned weights per evidence level is worth several F1 points on business data. That is an analogy, not a measurement on OSHA data.
- **Pitfalls:**
  1. Fellegi–Sunter assumes comparisons are conditionally independent, but here name, place-word and city are strongly correlated (the "city name as division name" failure). Model "place word in name equals the sub's city" as one combined level, not two independent agreements.
  2. EIN silver labels come only from ITA filers, a size- and industry-dependent subset, so m/u estimates for sole proprietors will be under-sampled. Reweight or add hand labels for that stratum.
  3. Do not run unsupervised EM on the "name" column alone, because Splink's README explicitly warns against this.
- **Tool ranking for this team:**
  1. Splink: native DuckDB, explains its decisions, supports labels and query-time matching.
  2. ING EMM: borrow its indexers and its legal-entity and rank features; its classifier needs labelled pairs, which the EIN silver set provides.
  3. dedupe: use its *active-learning labelling idea*. The library itself duplicates the project's blocking and is pandas-heavy.
  4. fastLink: methodological reference only (R).
  5. Zingg: Spark/Java overhead not justified at about 320k rows.
  6. Senzing: proprietary, and about 320k inspections exceeds the 100k free tier. Possibly useful as an outside benchmark on a sample.
  7. pyJedAI: research toolkit, low priority.

### Gaps
- No published head-to-head benchmark of Splink vs dedupe vs Zingg vs Senzing on *company-name* data was found. Comparisons available are vendor blogs.
- Splink 5 is "recently released" per the README, but its changes (API differences from Splink 4, e.g. whether `register_term_frequency_lookup` and `find_matches_to_new_records` keep the same signatures) were not checked.
- Senzing's company-name-specific logic (legal-suffix handling, organisation-name scoring) and current licence terms beyond the 100k evaluation tier were not documented in the pages retrieved.

## 2. Company-name-specific techniques: normalisers, char n-gram TF-IDF, IDF-weighted soft token similarity, phonetics, acronyms/initials, DBA, and published benchmarks

### Takeaway
The best-supported recipe for company names is: normalise (legal suffixes) → weight tokens by IDF → compare tokens softly so near-matching tokens still count. Cohen et al. (2003) found SoftTFIDF with Jaro-Winkler the best general name metric. ING's EMM and the Census MAMBA platform both combine word- and char-n-gram TF-IDF with a learned classifier. This IDF-weighted, token-aware approach is what fixes the "Smith Roofing ≈ Jones Roofing" problem that sank generic embeddings, because ROOFING gets a low weight and SMITH/JONES high ones. Learned name embeddings beat TF-IDF only with domain training data, and published gains come from neighbouring tasks.

### Cited Findings
- Cohen, Ravikumar & Fienberg (2003) compared edit-distance, fast heuristic, token-based and hybrid metrics on name matching. The best was a hybrid of TF-IDF weighting with Jaro-Winkler at the token level (SoftTFIDF), which extends cosine similarity so that nearly matching tokens count. Monge-Elkan was among the measures compared. — [Cohen et al. 2003, PDF](https://pubs.dbs.uni-leipzig.de/dc/files/Cohen2003Acomparisonofstringdistance.pdf); [Semantic Scholar entry](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Comparison-of-String-Distance-Metrics-for-Tasks-Cohen-Ravikumar/9578679e028777dd709881f938114aa59fbbf481)
- ING open-sourced `sparse_dot_topn`, which speeds up the sparse matrix multiplication behind TF-IDF cosine top-k search. With word-level tokens it did 2.0×10^14 name comparisons in a couple of hours on a Spark cluster. ING uses it internally for name matching. — [ING WBAA blog "Even faster string comparison"](https://medium.com/wbaa/even-faster-string-comparison-2778be7fe480); [ING "Matching company names at scale"](https://medium.com/wbaa/matching-company-names-at-scale-af20429a80c7); original technique write-up: [van den Berg, "Super Fast String Matching in Python" (2017)](https://bergvca.github.io/2017/10/14/super-fast-string-matching.html)
- ING EMM combines word TF-IDF, char n-gram TF-IDF and sorted-neighbourhood indexers ("complementary for selecting realistic name-pair candidates"). Its classifier uses string, rank and **legal-entity-form** features (e.g. "Ltd" vs "Co"). — [ING EMM GitHub](https://github.com/ing-bank/EntityMatchingModel)
- cleanco (Python) strips legal-form terms (`basename("Some Big Pharma, LLC")` returns "Some Big Pharma"). It can also infer the organisation type and likely countries from the suffix, e.g. "Oy" suggests Finland. — [cleanco on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/cleanco); [cleanco source](https://github.com/psolin/cleanco/blob/master/cleanco/clean.py)
- MAMBA uses 13 FEBRL comparators on business name and address, including Jaro, Jaro-Winkler, q-grams, positional q-grams, Levenshtein, LCS, cosine, Smith-Waterman and sorted Winkler. Many of them are highly collinear, which is why a random forest or regularised model beats an additive logit. Soundex is used only as a residual blocking key. — [Cuffe & Goldschlag 2018](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2018/CES-WP-18-46.pdf)
- Ditto (Li et al., PVLDB 14, 2021) fine-tunes BERT/RoBERTa-style models on serialised record pairs and outperformed the previous best by up to 29% F1. **On matching two company datasets of 789K and 412K records it reached 96.5% F1.** It matched earlier state-of-the-art results with at most half the labelled data. — [Ditto, PVLDB 2021](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol14/p50-li.pdf); [arXiv 2004.00584](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.00584)
- On learned name embeddings: CoSiNES, a contrastive Siamese network for entity standardisation (2023), beat a TF-IDF baseline by 10.46% top-1 retrieval accuracy. A 2023 paper on company-name disambiguation with recurrent Siamese networks reports they beat standard string-matching baselines *when enough labelled data are available*. — [CoSiNES arXiv 2306.03316](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.03316); [Disambiguation of Company names via Deep Recurrent Networks, arXiv 2303.05391](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05391); related: [CompanyName2Vec, arXiv 2201.04687](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.04687)
- In the 2026 CorpFam benchmark (section 7), name-based methods (exact, Jaccard, sequence ratio, TF-IDF char 3-gram, MiniLM embeddings) recover all *identical-name* pairs but only 64.3% of "visible" pairs (names differ but share distinctive tokens; best method was MiniLM) and 4.7% of "invisible" pairs. — [CorpFam, arXiv 2609.04269 (Sept 2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04269v1)

### Inferences
- **Descriptor-word variants (HAGERMAN vs HAGERMAN CONSTRUCTION).** IDF-weighted token-set similarity (SoftTFIDF or Monge-Elkan with IDF) handles this by design: CONSTRUCTION has very low IDF in a construction-only corpus, so dropping it costs almost nothing. **Recommendation:** split tokens into classes (legal suffix, generic descriptor such as CONSTRUCTION/CONTRACTING/SERVICES/COMPANY/GROUP, trade word, place word, person-name token, number, distinctive). Compute IDF per class over establishments, not inspections. Score name similarity as *distinctive-token agreement* plus *penalties*: a trade-word **conflict** is penalised heavily, a missing descriptor lightly, and a place-word difference triggers the family logic in section 7. This is SoftTFIDF with class-specific weights. It can be estimated from EIN silver pairs as a small logistic model, or encoded as Splink comparison levels.
- **"Smith Roofing ≈ Jones Roofing" (why embeddings failed).** IDF-weighted lexical similarity scores this pair low because the only shared token is low-IDF. That is why char/word TF-IDF beats off-the-shelf sentence embeddings for *scoring* here. Embeddings remain plausible as an *extra blocking route*: in CorpFam, MiniLM was the best method on "visible" pairs. Always re-score embedding candidates lexically.
- **Candidate recall (0.95).** Add a char 3-gram TF-IDF top-k indexer (`sparse_dot_topn`, or DuckDB SQL on n-gram tables) and a sorted-neighbourhood pass on the name core, alongside rarest-token blocking. Treat the union as candidates and measure each route's marginal recall by stratum. EMM's experience is that these indexers complement each other.
- **Legal-suffix handling.** The project's SQL macros already strip suffixes. cleanco's suffix list and its entity-type output are a cheap check of macro coverage. Following EMM, keep the stripped suffix as a *feature*: LLC vs INC disagreement is weak negative evidence and can be learned.
- **Phonetics.** MAMBA and CorpFam use Soundex only for blocking. Phonetic codes such as Double Metaphone or NYSIIS are built for person surnames, so use them only on tokens flagged as person names (sole proprietors) and as an extra blocking key, never as an agreement level on whole company names.
- **Initials and acronyms ("ABC" vs "ALLIED BUILDING CONTRACTORS").** Generate an initials key from the first letters of the distinctive tokens and add it as a blocking key. Allow an acronym match only with address or zip agreement, because three-letter acronyms are highly ambiguous.
- **DBA / "the X company".** Split on DBA, D/B/A, T/A, AKA and "TRADING AS" into two aliases, each matched separately, with the establishment taking the better score. Strip a leading "THE" and trailing "COMPANY/CO" (already a descriptor class).
- **Ditto's 96.5% F1 on company data** suggests a fine-tuned cross-encoder over (name, city, state, zip, address) pairs can reach high accuracy when labelled pairs exist. The project's EIN pairs (tens of thousands) are that resource (see section 4).

### Gaps
- No public, recent (2023–2026) benchmark dedicated to *US company-name* matching (as opposed to products/citations) with standard splits was found. Ditto's company dataset is not described in the retrieved snippet.
- Specific Python implementations of SoftTFIDF/Monge-Elkan (e.g. py_stringmatching) and of phonetic codes (e.g. jellyfish, abydos) were not verified this session.
- No sources were retrieved on DBA parsing, acronym expansion or "the X company" patterns for company names. The recommendations above are engineering judgement.
- A "basic-normalizer" library was not found or verified.

## 3. Address handling: parsing/standardisation, geocoding, open address data, and how to weight address agreement (mailing vs site, shared offices/registered agents)

### Takeaway
Census evidence says address agreement matters more than name strictness for business linkage accuracy. For OSHA construction data, though, the inspection *site* address is a job site that many unrelated firms share, so address evidence must be frequency-weighted, the same TF idea as for names. Free building blocks: usaddress or libpostal for parsing, the Census Geocoder batch API for standardised coordinates and Census geographies, and Overture/OpenAddresses as an open address-point reference (US coverage partial, alpha).

### Cited Findings
- Kim & McCue (2020): "using relatively strict matching on address is important for match accuracy, while results are less sensitive to name matching strictness." — [CES WP 20-03 abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cen/wpaper/20-03.html)
- In MAMBA's additive logit, the name coefficient was 8.125 and the address coefficient 4.463 (with interaction: name 6.044, address 1.396, interaction 4.096). Both carry substantial weight, and their interaction matters. Blocking is by zip5, then city, then zip3, then state. — [Cuffe & Goldschlag 2018](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2018/CES-WP-18-46.pdf)
- The Census Geocoder (free) accepts single addresses, batches of up to 10,000 addresses (5 MB per file) or coordinates. Larger jobs must be split. Results can be tied to different "benchmarks" (data snapshots, generally made twice a year). — [Census Geocoder FAQ](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/maps-data/data/Census_Geocoder_FAQ.pdf); [Geocoding Services API doc](https://geocoding.geo.census.gov/geocoder/Geocoding_Services_API.pdf); [Census Geocoder documentation](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/geography/technical-documentation/complete-technical-documentation/census-geocoder.html); Python wrapper [censusgeocode on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/censusgeocode/)
- libpostal is a C library trained on about 50 million addresses across 100+ countries. It uses CRF-based parsing, needs a download of a few GB and about 2 GB of RAM, and handles abbreviations well ("St", "Blvd"). usaddress is a lighter, Python-native, CRF-based parser for US addresses only. A 2023 Deepparse paper reports Deepparse and libpostal near-perfect and comparable on parsing. — [libhunt comparison](https://www.libhunt.com/compare-libpostal-vs-usaddress); [usaddress on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/usaddress/0.2.6); [Deepparse, NLP-OSS 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.nlposs-1.3.pdf); [Senzing explainer on libpostal](https://senzing.com/what-is-libpostal/)
- Overture's addresses theme draws on 175+ sources, mainly OpenAddresses and AddressForAll plus authoritative public datasets. The US is only partly covered, with 125,367,277 US address points. The theme is in **alpha**. The schema is heavily based on OpenAddresses. — [Overture blog "Building the Overture Addresses Data Theme" (2025)](https://overturemaps.org/blog/2025/building-the-overture-addresses-data-theme/); [Overture addresses guide](https://docs.overturemaps.org/guides/addresses/)

### Inferences
- **Weight address evidence by frequency, not just agreement.** Build an "address distinctiveness" table: for each standardised address key (or geocoded point rounded to about 10 m), count the distinct name cores seen there. Job sites (many subs per site), multi-tenant office buildings and registered-agent addresses then get low weight automatically, exactly as common names do. In Splink, register this as the address TF table.
- **Mailing vs site address.** The GC-entered city/state almost certainly describes the sub's office. OSHA inspection site fields describe where work happened (the job site), and any mailing fields describe the firm. **Recommendation:** compare the sub's location primarily against OSHA *mailing* address fields where present. Treat *site*-address agreement between two OSHA records as weak, frequency-discounted evidence, and between the GC entry and an OSHA site as near-useless except as a "works in this metro" prior. A city mismatch with the site city should not be strong negative evidence either, since subs travel.
- **Candidate route "records at already-matched addresses".** This one-hop graph expansion is the riskiest route under the frequency view. Restrict it to addresses whose distinct-name count is below a small threshold (e.g. ≤3) and to mailing-type addresses.
- **Tooling.** Parse with usaddress (pure Python, US-only, fits a DuckDB UDF or a pre-processing pass). Reserve libpostal for when international or very messy strings appear, given its 2 GB RAM cost. Batch-geocode distinct establishment addresses through the Census Geocoder (about 195k distinct names means fewer distinct mailing addresses; at 10k per batch that is a few dozen batch calls, a one-time job refreshed with each data load). Store lat/long, a match-quality flag and the census tract/block. Use a geodistance band (same point / under 1 km / same county) as an address comparison level that tolerates "STE 200" vs "SUITE 200" and street-abbreviation noise. Overture/OpenAddresses can add a stable address-point ID where covered, but is alpha with partial US coverage, so it is optional.
- **Pitfall.** Geocoding can make two different suites in one tower agree at point level. Keep unit/suite as a separate comparison level, and do not let geocode agreement override a confident name disagreement.

### Gaps
- USPS CASS certification and USPS address APIs were not researched (terms, cost, rate limits).
- No source was found quantifying how often OSHA construction records carry usable mailing addresses distinct from site addresses. Measure this in the warehouse.
- No published study was found on false-match rates caused by registered-agent or shared-office addresses in business linkage.

## 4. Learning-based and LLM-based entity matching (2021–2026): Ditto/Unicorn/AnyMatch, Peeters–Bizer LLM studies, Match/Compare/Select, costs, calibration, LLM-generated labels, cross-encoders vs embeddings

### Takeaway
Three recent findings bear on Jev's design:
- Fine-tuned *small cross-encoders* remain the most cost-effective scorers. A July 2026 factorial study finds cross-encoders beat bi-encoders and that bigger models rely more on shortcuts.
- Large LLMs add value mainly under distribution shift and with zero or few labels.
- A *list-wise "Select"* prompt (one query, many candidates) beats pairwise "Match" prompts and costs less.

For this project: keep a small fine-tuned scorer (Jev, possibly upgraded to a cross-encoder) as the default. Use the LLM in Select mode over each sub's candidate list for the uncertain tail. Calibrate both on held-out labels. Use an LLM as a *teacher* to label training pairs; Steiner & Bizer (2026) show LLM labels come within 2 F1 points of human labels at a tiny fraction of the cost.

### Cited Findings
- **Narayan et al., "Can Foundation Models Wrangle Your Data?" (PVLDB 16(4), 2022):** with hand-curated demonstrations, GPT-3-175B reached state-of-the-art on 4 entity-matching benchmarks, framed as "Are A and B the same? Yes/No". — [VLDB PDF](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol16/p738-narayan.pdf); [arXiv 2205.09911](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.09911)
- **Peeters, Steiner & Bizer, "Entity Matching using Large Language Models" (EDBT 2025; arXiv v4 Oct 2024):** "the best LLMs require no or only a few training examples to perform comparably to PLMs that were fine-tuned using thousands of examples". LLM matchers are more robust to unseen entities. "There is no single best prompt"; prompts must be tuned per model and dataset. GPT-4 can produce structured explanations and help identify the causes of matching errors. — [arXiv 2310.11244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11244); earlier: [Using ChatGPT for Entity Matching (2023)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.03423)
- **Steiner, Peeters & Bizer, "Fine-tuning LLMs for Entity Matching" (ICDE Workshops 2025; arXiv 2024):** fine-tuning clearly helps smaller models, with mixed results for larger ones. It improves generalisation to in-domain datasets but *hurts cross-domain transfer*. Training examples were augmented with LLM-generated explanations, and LLMs were used to select and generate examples. — [arXiv 2409.08185](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.08185)
- **Steiner & Bizer, "Labeling Training Data for Entity Matching Using Large Language Models" (arXiv, June 2026):** an LLM teacher labels pairs to train a smaller student. Labelling five benchmark training sets with GPT-5.2 cost $28.31–$40.88 each, against an estimated 470 hours of manual labelling. Students trained on LLM labels came within 2 F1 points of those trained on human labels. "Ditto is 41.5 to 534 times faster than directly using an LLM" at inference. — [arXiv 2606.28823](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28823)
- **Wang et al., "Match, Compare, or Select?" (COLING 2025; arXiv 2405.16884):** compares pairwise matching, comparing (an anchor against two candidates) and selecting (an anchor against a candidate list, pick the best). With GPT-3.5-Turbo, selecting averaged about 81.6% F1 vs 64.0% for matching across 8 datasets (10 candidates per record from Sparkly blocking). The compound ComEM framework (a medium open model such as Flan-T5-XL filters and ranks, then a stronger LLM selects from the top-k) reached 85.6%. Selecting cost $1.71 vs $4.52 for matching (less than half the API calls). Selecting suffers *position bias*: accuracy drops when the true match appears later in the list. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16884); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.8.pdf)
- **AnyMatch (Zhang, Groth, Calixto, Schelter; arXiv 2409.04073, 2024):** a small fine-tuned language model for *zero-shot* EM on unseen datasets. Training data is built with an AutoML filter for hard pairs, attribute-level examples and label-imbalance control. It reached F1 81.96, second-best against 13 baselines including GPT-4-class models, at a reported 3,899× lower inference cost. — [arXiv 2409.04073](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04073)
- **Unicorn (Tu et al., SIGMOD 2023):** a single multi-task model (encoder + mixture-of-experts + matcher) for entity matching, entity linking, string matching, schema matching and similar tasks, with zero-shot prediction on new tasks. It contrasts with DeepMatcher/Ditto, which need hundreds of labelled pairs per dataset. — [Unicorn GitHub](https://github.com/ruc-datalab/Unicorn); [SIGMOD Record summary](https://sigmodrecord.org/publications/sigmodRecord/2403/pdfs/12_unicorn-fan.pdf)
- **Zhang, Li, Calixto, Groth, Schelter, "Beyond Scale and Generation" (arXiv 2607.24688, July 2026):** 1,215 fine-tuning runs. Cross-encoders consistently beat bi-encoders. For bi-encoders, embedding-oriented model variants are a better starting point. Generative matchers help mainly under *distribution shift* (unseen schema differences, cross-dataset transfer). Larger models do not reliably do better and lean more on shortcut learning. — [arXiv 2607.24688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24688)
- **Structure-guided fine-tuning (ACL 2026 Industry, arXiv 2605.23597):** for *person-name* matching, a two-phase curriculum (first learn to parse name structure, then learn to match) reached 99.02% accuracy and 0.994 F1 on 50k real pairs. It beat GPT-4o few-shot and single-stage fine-tuning and is in production at Dream11. — [arXiv 2605.23597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23597)
- **Calibration:** Kamsteeg et al. (UncertaiNLP @ EMNLP 2025) studied a RoBERTa-based matcher on Abt-Buy, DBLP-ACM, iTunes-Amazon and a **Company** dataset. Expected calibration error was 0.0043–0.0552, meaning slight overconfidence. Temperature scaling cut it by up to 23.83%, beating MC-dropout and ensembles. — [arXiv 2509.19557](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19557). Broader work finds LLM verbalised confidences are often miscalibrated, and Platt/temperature-style post-hoc scaling is a common fix. — e.g. [Calibrating Verbalized Probabilities for LLMs, arXiv 2410.06707](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.06707)

### Inferences
- **Match the LLM strategy to the per-sub design.** The adjudicator sees one subcontractor and several candidate establishments, which is exactly the "Select" setting. **Recommendation:** for UNCERTAIN subs, send the sub plus *all* its uncertain candidates in one structured-output call: "for each candidate ID: same legal entity / same corporate family / different / can't tell, with evidence IDs". This also solves the transitive-consistency problem across that sub's candidates. Counter position bias by shuffling candidate order and, for high-stakes cases, running twice with reversed order and requiring agreement. Wang et al. report fewer calls and higher F1 for Select than pairwise Match.
- **ComEM-style cascade for cost:**
  - Jev (or a fine-tuned cross-encoder) handles everything and ranks candidates.
  - Only subs where Jev's calibrated P(same) falls between thresholds go to the LLM.
  - The LLM sees only the top-k candidates.

  This keeps LLM spend proportional to the 28% uncertain tail.
- **Upgrade path for Jev.** Per "Beyond Scale and Generation", a small cross-encoder (MiniLM/DeBERTa-small class) fine-tuned on serialised pairs is the strongest cost-efficient scorer, and bigger is not better. Input: `name | city | state | zip | address | trade/NAICS`, plus the project's engineered features as text tags (e.g. `[DISTINCTIVENESS=3] [PERSON_NAME] [TRADE_CONFLICT]`, following Ditto's "inject domain knowledge" idea). Train it on EIN silver pairs plus LLM-teacher labels (Steiner & Bizer 2026) for strata the EIN labels under-cover (sole proprietors, small firms). Hold out by *state* or by *firm* to test the cross-dataset shift that hurts fine-tuned models.
- **Calibration is mandatory before thresholding.** Fit temperature or Platt/isotonic scaling for Jev and for any LLM probability on a held-out labelled set, and report reliability curves. Prefer category outputs (same / family / different / unsure) mapped to calibrated probabilities over the LLM's raw verbalised percentage.
- **Embeddings revisited.** The earlier rejection is consistent with the literature: bi-encoders (embeddings) lose to cross-encoders, and generic sentence embeddings over-weight shared generic words. A *contrastively fine-tuned* name encoder trained on EIN pairs with hard negatives (e.g. "Smith Roofing" vs "Jones Roofing", same core with different EINs) could become a good *blocking* route. Use it only for candidate retrieval, never as the decision score.
- **Structure first, then match.** The ACL 2026 curriculum result for person names suggests a cheap analogue: have the model, or the existing SQL, first label each name's parts (core / descriptor / trade / place / person-name / legal suffix / DBA alias), then match on the parsed structure. This directly targets the person-name and place-word failure modes.
- **Never show safety history to the adjudicator.** This is already project policy, and nothing in the literature argues against it.

### Gaps
- None of the LLM-EM benchmarks above uses US government enforcement records or construction firms. Transfer of the reported F1 numbers is unknown.
- The 2026 papers on cost-efficient RAG for EM ([arXiv 2602.05708](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.05708)) and structured multi-step reasoning for EM ([arXiv 2511.22832](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.22832)) were found but not read.
- No source compared Claude-family models specifically on EM in 2025–2026. Peeters et al. tested GPT-4 and open models, and Steiner & Bizer used GPT-5.2.

## 5. Graph and clustering approaches: transitive closure risks, correlation clustering, shared-attribute graph edges, incremental/query-time ER

### Takeaway
The per-subcontractor, resolve-on-demand design avoids the main graph risk, chaining through transitive closure, because each query yields a star-shaped decision rather than global clusters. The literature and tools support this query-time pattern (Splink `find_matches_to_new_records`). Graph edges from shared addresses or phones are useful *retrieval* signals, especially for corporate families. As match evidence they must be frequency-discounted.

### Cited Findings
- Splink's `find_matches_to_new_records` scores only new-vs-existing pairs and returns ranked matches, which suits operational or real-time linkage. Fully incremental cluster maintenance was described as not easily supported. — [Splink real-time linkage demo](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/demos/examples/duckdb/real_time_record_linkage.html); [Splink discussion #869](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink/discussions/869)
- Binette & Steorts' review "(Almost) All of Entity Resolution" (arXiv 2008.04443; *Science Advances* 2022) surveys the pipeline (blocking, matching, clustering and canonicalisation) and the statistical issues in going from pairwise decisions to entities. — [arXiv 2008.04443](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.04443)
- FlexER (arXiv 2209.07569) frames ER as serving *multiple resolution intents* at once, for example "same entity" vs coarser groupings, rather than a single notion of a match. — [FlexER arXiv 2209.07569](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07569)
- Graph-based active learning has been proposed for *repairing* entity clusters after pairwise matching. — [Graph-based Active Learning for Entity Cluster Repair, arXiv 2401.14992](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.14992)
- In CorpFam (2026), attribute-keyed blocking (zip, city/state) and embedding nearest-neighbour blocking each retrieved under 3% of "invisible" corporate-family links, and the union of seven blocking schemes reached only 6.8%. — [CorpFam arXiv 2609.04269](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04269v1)
- MAMBA runs blocks in order (zip5, then city, then zip3, then state, then residual), so records matched in one block are no longer at risk in later blocks. — [Cuffe & Goldschlag 2018](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2018/CES-WP-18-46.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Keep query-time ER as the core.** Do not build global fuzzy clusters in the warehouse. This is consistent with the team's own finding that global fuzzy merging was wrong 1 time in 6, and with the general risk that chained pairwise links (A~B, B~C) merge unrelated firms through a common name or shared job-site address.
- If cross-establishment clusters are ever needed (e.g. a reusable "firm" entity across GCs), cluster *only* above a high threshold, cap cluster diameter (every member must score ≥ τ against the cluster's highest-confidence representative, not merely be chained), and prefer correlation-clustering-style objectives over connected components. Re-check clusters with the labelling queue ("cluster repair").
- Treat shared mailing address, shared phone (if ever available) and shared officer as *edges for retrieval and family detection*. Treat them as *evidence of same entity* only after frequency discounting (section 3).
- Use the Select-mode adjudicator (section 4) as the local consistency mechanism. A sub matched to establishments A and B implies A~B, so the adjudicator should see A and B together.

### Gaps
- No specific source on correlation clustering for company ER was retrieved this session.
- Splink's clustering function semantics (connected components vs alternatives) in Splink 5 were not checked.

## 6. Evaluation and labelling: building a gold set efficiently, estimating precision/recall with silver labels, handling label noise

### Takeaway
Precision and recall from convenient benchmarks are biased and usually over-optimistic. The ER-evaluation literature (Binette et al., PatentsView) recommends sampling *entities or queries* from the real population and using estimators that correct for sampling design. For this project: keep EIN silver labels for *training*, but estimate production precision and recall on a stratified, query-level gold set of GC-style subs. Label it with an LLM-assisted, active-learning queue, and report metrics per stratum (distinctiveness, person-name, descriptor variant, multi-EIN family) rather than one aggregate.

### Cited Findings
- Computing precision and recall on small benchmark datasets "has been shown to yield biased and over-optimistic performance assessments in ER, potentially leading to performance rank reversals". Binette provides a sampling methodology and tailored estimators that correct for sampling bias. Applied to PatentsView inventor disambiguation, they estimated precision between 79% and 91%. — [PatentsView "Estimating the Performance of ER Algorithms"](https://patentsview.org/data-in-action/estimating-performance-entity-resolution-algorithms-lessons-learned-through); [arXiv 2210.01230](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01230)
- Tooling: the ER-Evaluation Python package (*JOSS* 8(91):5619, 2023), PatentsView-Evaluation datasets and tools ([arXiv 2301.03591](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.03591)), and Binette & Reiter's "How to Evaluate Entity Resolution Systems" ([arXiv 2404.05622](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.05622), 2024). — [ER-Evaluation JOSS](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.05619.pdf); [PatentsView-Evaluation GitHub](https://github.com/patentsview/patentsview-evaluation)
- MAMBA built its training and evaluation data by clerical review of about 2,000 records, sampling equally from score bands 0.5–0.7, 0.7–0.9 and 0.9–1 to get enough rare matches. Results were reported from 10-fold cross-validation to limit over-fitting on small samples. — [Cuffe & Goldschlag 2018](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2018/CES-WP-18-46.pdf)
- dedupe and Zingg use active learning: the learner picks the next most informative pairs to label. — [Tilores blog (vendor)](https://tilores.io/content/best-open-source-entity-resolution-and-record-linkage-libraries-splink-zingg-dedupe-and-when-to-move-beyond-them); [Zingg GitHub](https://github.com/zinggAI/zingg)
- LLM-teacher labelling cost $28–41 per benchmark training set against about 470 manual hours, with downstream F1 within 2 points of human labels. — [Steiner & Bizer, arXiv 2606.28823 (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28823)
- CorpFam shows that an aggregate F1 (76.8) hid near-zero performance on the hardest stratum, and argues that stratified (per-stratum) recall is the meaningful metric. — [CorpFam arXiv 2609.04269](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04269v1)

### Inferences
- **Biases in the silver labels.** These are inferences; verify them in the data.
  1. ITA filers are a non-random subset that skews to larger, more stable establishments, so sole proprietors and tiny subs, the "common person-name" failure, are under-represented.
  2. The negatives "same name core + state, different EIN" mix truly different firms with *same-family* firms under different EINs (D.R. Horton divisions) and with one firm that changed EIN (re-incorporation, a new LLC). This makes measured precision a *lower bound* for "same corporate family" and possibly noisy even for "same legal entity".
  3. Positives "same EIN" can join establishments with different names (a DBA or rebrand) that a name matcher would rightly struggle with, which deflates candidate recall in a way the product may not care about.
- **Recommended gold-set protocol** (sized for a small team):
  1. *Unit = query*: one GC-style subcontractor input (name, city, state, optional trade/licence). Draw queries from real GC entries if available. Otherwise sample OSHA names and perturb them the way GCs type (dropping the descriptor, abbreviating, adding LLC, using the office city).
  2. *Stratify* by distinctiveness bucket × person-name flag × descriptor-variant flag × multi-EIN-family flag × state. Oversample the failure strata.
  3. For each query, label *every* candidate the pipeline (and a deliberately loose blocker) returns, on a 4-level scale: same establishment / same legal entity / same corporate family / unrelated, plus "insufficient info". This gives precision and candidate recall per level.
  4. *Pre-label with an LLM in Select mode* (section 4), then have a human confirm. Send disagreements between LLM, Jev and rules to the human first, which is the active-learning heuristic.
  5. Report precision and recall per stratum with design-weighted estimators (Binette-style) and confidence intervals. Several hundred labelled queries should give usable intervals for the main strata. That figure is a rough judgement, not from a source.
- **Use the silver labels better.** Treat "same EIN" as positive for *legal entity*. Re-label "different EIN but same distinctive core + same mailing address / same HQ city" as *family (ambiguous)* and exclude those pairs from precision denominators at the legal-entity level, or report them separately. This should directly change the 0.85 headline, and in which direction depends on product policy (section 7).
- **The existing held-out check (seeds 7/11)** is good practice. Add a *temporal* holdout (train ≤2023, test 2024–2026) and a *state* holdout to test the distribution shift that hurts fine-tuned matchers.

### Gaps
- Binette's estimators target clustering and disambiguation over a whole database. Adapting them to *query-time* matching (per-query precision and recall) needs a small methodological step; no source doing exactly this was found.
- No source quantified the size or industry bias of OSHA ITA filers relative to all OSHA-inspected construction firms (worth computing in the warehouse).

## 7. Corporate families: "same company" vs "related company" in ER literature and practice

### Takeaway
Recent work (CorpFam, Sept 2026) argues that corporate-family resolution is a *different task* from entity matching, a retrieval problem whose evidence often appears in neither record. Name similarity recovers family links only when the names share distinctive tokens. The project's D.R. Horton-style failures are the "visible" kind, where names share a distinctive token. These can be detected and should be surfaced as a separate *RELATED / same-family* outcome with hierarchical labels, not forced into MATCHED or EXCLUDED.

### Cited Findings
- CorpFam (Gupta, arXiv 2609.04269, 2 Sept 2026): 14,288 parent-child links across 10,307 families, from 6.64M US federal award records where suppliers self-report their ultimate parent; 54,864 candidate pairs. Strata:
  - identical names after normalisation, 55.0% of pairs (97.3% are positives);
  - "visible", names differ but share distinctive tokens, 21.5%;
  - "invisible", no shared distinctive tokens, 23.5% (16.9% are positives).

  Best recall was 100% for identical, 64.3% for visible (MiniLM) and 4.7% for invisible. 93.2% of invisible links never enter any candidate set, and the union of 7 blockers reaches 6.8%. SEC Exhibit 21 subsidiary lists corroborated 64.2% of invisible links (95% CI 60.8–67.5). 94.1% of parents never transact, so they have no registered address, and attribute-based models stay near the majority-class baseline on the invisible stratum. Conclusion: family resolution is "retrieval, not matching", and aggregate F1 misleads. — [CorpFam arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04269v1); [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.04269)
- FlexER models multiple resolution "intents" (e.g. a strict same-entity intent and a looser grouping intent) in one framework, so the same pair can be labelled differently under each intent. — [FlexER arXiv 2209.07569](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07569)
- ING EMM's "name aggregation" combines match scores across several names belonging to one account, a practical way to handle one real-world party appearing under several names. — [ING EMM GitHub](https://github.com/ing-bank/EntityMatchingModel)
- Commercial ER engines (e.g. Senzing) present both *matches* and *relationships / possible matches* between entities rather than a binary answer. — [Senzing get-started](https://senzing.com/explore-senzing-entity-resolution/)

### Inferences
- **Hierarchical labels and outputs.** Adopt the 4-level label (same establishment ⊂ same legal entity ⊂ same family ⊂ unrelated) in silver labels, gold labels and pipeline outputs. Add a **RELATED (same family)** status next to MATCHED / UNCERTAIN / EXCLUDED. In the UI it can show the family's OSHA history separately and clearly labelled. Whether family history should count toward a sub's risk is a product decision. Since GCs contract with a specific legal entity, the default should probably be "show, don't merge". That default is a judgement call.
- **Detecting the visible-family pattern (D.R. Horton divisions; city name as division name):**
  - the distinctive core agrees and is rare at national level (e.g. HORTON);
  - the names differ only in place words, division words or descriptors;
  - the EINs differ, or the mailing addresses differ by metro.

  Treat this as same family, not a match, unless the place word equals the sub's entered city (then it is likely the right division, so MATCHED with that division only). This turns the current "city names used as division names" failure into a rule *or* a Fellegi–Sunter level, "core agree + place token ≠ sub city", with its own m/u.
- **Family evidence beyond names (optional, later).** For publicly traded parents (large homebuilders and GCs), SEC Exhibit 21 subsidiary lists are a free, document-based way to retrieve family links, and CorpFam validated invisible links against them. Shared mailing address or HQ across OSHA records is another family-level edge (section 5). Both are *retrieval* aids, not match evidence.
- **Effect on evaluation.** Re-scoring the EIN silver negatives with a family tier will likely show that some of the current "false positives" behind precision 0.85 are same-family pairs. Report legal-entity precision and family-level precision separately so improvements are not misread.

#### Cross-cutting priority order (all inferences; ties each step to a listed failure mode)
1. **Hierarchical labels + stratified query-level gold set** (sections 6 and 7). Fixes evaluation noise from multi-EIN families and makes every later change measurable. Low cost.
2. **Token-class-aware, IDF-weighted name similarity (SoftTFIDF-style) computed over establishments** (section 2). Targets descriptor variants (HAGERMAN vs HAGERMAN CONSTRUCTION) and "Smith vs Jones Roofing". Low to medium cost, pure DuckDB/Python.
3. **Fellegi–Sunter scoring layer (Splink on DuckDB, or the same maths in SQL), with m from EIN pairs, u from random sampling and TF from establishment-level tables, including an address-frequency table** (sections 1 and 3). Targets the 28% uncertain bucket, calibration, common person names (TF on full name + address/zip requirement) and shared-address false matches. Medium cost.
4. **Family tier / RELATED outcome with place-word-vs-sub-city logic** (section 7). Targets D.R. Horton divisions and city-as-division names. Low to medium cost.
5. **Extra candidate routes: char 3-gram TF-IDF top-k (`sparse_dot_topn`), sorted neighbourhood, initials key** (section 2). Targets the remaining 5% candidate-recall gap. Measure marginal recall per route.
6. **Mailing-address standardisation (usaddress) + Census Geocoder batch geocoding; down-weight site addresses** (section 3). Medium cost, one-off plus refresh.
7. **Adjudicator upgrades: LLM in Select mode over each sub's candidate list with order shuffling; Jev upgraded to a small fine-tuned cross-encoder trained on EIN pairs + LLM-teacher labels; temperature/Platt calibration of both** (section 4). Targets the uncertain tail at controlled cost.

### Gaps
- No ER source was found that specifically handles US *construction* corporate families (homebuilder divisions, regional LLCs per state). The D.R. Horton pattern is reasoned from the project's own failure list, not from literature.
- Whether OSHA records the same EIN-less firm under a parent's name vs a division's name consistently across area offices is unknown and would need an empirical check in the warehouse.
